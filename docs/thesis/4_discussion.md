# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Interpretation of Results

The comparative evaluation across 17,317 masked slots and 35 stratified test devices produces three findings that are individually clear and collectively coherent. This section interprets each in depth, moving from the raw numbers to the structural explanations that make them legible.

### 4.1.1 Why TimesFM Dominates

TimesFM achieves an overall MAE of 1.18 dB — a margin of 1.52 dB below the next-best method (Combined, 2.70 dB) and 2.36 dB below the worst-performing statistical method (KNN, 3.54 dB). The improvement is not marginal: it is large enough to shift imputed values from above the perceptual threshold of 3 dB — where differences are clearly audible — to below the just-noticeable difference of approximately 1 dB. This is a qualitative change in imputation fidelity, not a refinement within the same quality tier.

The explanation lies in what each method can represent. The statistical methods — historical median and spatial KNN — operate on hand-crafted assumptions about the structure of urban noise: that it repeats by hour-of-day across days, and that it correlates with nearby sensors at the same timestamp. Both assumptions are approximately true on average but systematically violated in the specific cases where imputation is most needed. A network outage silences a device during an anomalous event — a street festival, a road closure, a severe weather period — when the historical median of ordinary days provides a poor approximation. Similarly, KNN fails precisely when the neighbours needed most are also missing.

TimesFM does not rely on either assumption. As a decoder-only transformer pre-trained on a large and diverse corpus of real-world time series, the model has learned representations of temporal dynamics — trend, seasonality, level shifts, transient spikes — that are domain-general rather than hand-specified. Urban SPL data shares structural features with many time series domains present in the pre-training corpus: it exhibits clear diurnal rhythms comparable to retail foot traffic or energy consumption, weekly cycles analogous to business calendars, and level shifts analogous to seasonal regime changes in weather or climate data. The model can therefore transfer these learned representations to the acoustic domain without any task-specific training or fine-tuning.

Critically, the performance advantage is consistent across all three evaluation groups: TimesFM's MAE varies by only 0.06 dB across Groups A, B, and C (1.14 to 1.20 dB). The statistical methods, in contrast, vary by 0.47 dB (Historical) and 1.56 dB (KNN) across the same groups. This group-to-group stability is the strongest evidence that TimesFM's advantage is not a dataset-specific artefact — it reflects a genuine robustness to the structural conditions (spatial isolation, history length) that selectively degrade the statistical methods.

The zero-shot nature of the result deserves emphasis. TimesFM was not fine-tuned on Tallinn SPL data. The 1.18 dB MAE is achieved entirely through generalisation from a pre-training corpus that contains no acoustic sensor measurements. This establishes a lower bound: with domain-specific fine-tuning on a held-out subset of the Tallinn dataset, the model's accuracy would almost certainly improve further, particularly for sensors in atypical acoustic environments such as tunnels or parks whose temporal patterns may differ from the majority of the pre-training corpus.

### 4.1.2 Why KNN Underperforms in Group A

The most counterintuitive result in the evaluation is that KNN performs *worse* in Group A (Connected) than in Group B (Isolated): KNN achieves a MAE of 4.12 dB in the connected group compared to 3.20 dB in the isolated group, despite Group A devices having many close neighbours and long operational histories. Group A was designed to represent favourable conditions for KNN; Group B was designed to stress it. The reversal requires explanation.

The key is the distinction between *spatial isolation* and *correlated missingness*. The isolation score used to stratify devices measures the fraction of a device's missing hours that KNN could not fill from neighbours — that is, hours where all neighbours within 1 km were simultaneously absent. Group A devices have low isolation scores precisely because, when their data is missing, their neighbours typically have data. But the evaluation masks slots drawn from a device's *original readings* — timestamps when the device itself was operational.

In Group A, devices are densely deployed along shared network infrastructure: multiple sensors on the same gateway, the same LoRaWAN repeater, or the same administrative segment of the city's IoT platform. When a device in this group loses connectivity — due to a gateway failure, a firmware update, or a platform-level outage — all devices sharing that infrastructure lose connectivity simultaneously. At the specific timestamps when Group A devices are missing, their neighbours are therefore also likely to be missing, leaving KNN without usable input.

Historical imputation is immune to this failure mode: its lookback values come from prior days, not from concurrent sensors, and are structurally unaffected by simultaneous outages. The 0.73 dB advantage of Historical over KNN in Group A confirms that correlated missingness — not spatial separation — is the dominant driver of KNN's failure in dense urban IoT deployments.

This finding has a broader implication for sensor network design. The conventional assumption that spatial redundancy improves data completeness holds when failures are independent. In IoT deployments where sensors share network infrastructure, failures are correlated, and spatial imputation methods inherit this correlation as a systematic weakness. The evaluation's Group A result is an empirical demonstration of this general principle, grounded in the specific architecture of Tallinn's acoustic monitoring network.

### 4.1.3 Why Combined Always Beats Its Components

The Combined method consistently outperforms both the Historical median and the spatial KNN across all three evaluation groups. In Group A, it achieves a MAE of 2.98 dB — improving on Historical (3.39 dB) by 12% and on KNN (4.12 dB) by 28%. In Group B, it achieves 2.53 dB, improving on both KNN (3.20 dB) and Historical (3.43 dB). In Group C, it achieves 2.23 dB, improving on KNN (2.56 dB) and Historical (2.92 dB).

The explanation lies in the mechanics of inverse-variance weighting. The Combined method does not average the two source estimates with fixed weights. Instead, for each individual slot, it assigns higher weight to whichever source has a more internally consistent sample set — lower variance in its lookback window (for Historical) or neighbour pool (for KNN). When the historical lookback contains ten closely clustered values, Historical receives high weight; when the neighbour pool is dense and consistent, KNN receives high weight. The blend is adaptive rather than averaging.

This slot-level adaptivity explains why Combined outperforms even the better of its two components in any given group. Across a fleet of 471 sensors with heterogeneous acoustic environments, the relative reliability of Historical and KNN varies not just across devices but across individual missing slots within the same device. A sensor on a transit artery may have a very consistent historical window during morning peak hours (making Historical highly reliable) but a variable neighbour pool at the same hour (making KNN less reliable) — and the weights shift accordingly. A sensor in a residential zone during nighttime hours may exhibit the reverse pattern. No fixed choice between the two methods can capture this slot-level variation; only a per-slot adaptive blend can.

The performance gap between Combined and its best individual component is smallest in Group C (0.33 dB) and largest in Group A (0.84 dB). In Group C, both Historical and KNN perform well because short-history devices may have simpler acoustic environments with lower temporal variance, making both sources relatively reliable. When both sources are reliable and produce similar estimates, blending adds little. In Group A, the correlated-outage pattern makes KNN unreliable for many slots while leaving Historical unaffected — precisely the condition where the adaptive blend adds the most value, by routing weight away from KNN toward Historical on the problematic slots.

## 4.2 Practical Implications for Urban Noise Monitoring

### 4.2.1 WHO Tier Classification Accuracy

The WHO environmental noise guidelines organise acoustic exposure into tiers separated by 10 dB boundaries: below 45 dB (safe), 45–54 dB (marginally acceptable), 55–64 dB (moderate risk), 65–74 dB (high risk), and 75 dB and above (very high risk). These tiers are used in policy decisions including building permit regulation, noise abatement zone designation, and health impact assessment. A misclassification — an imputed value that falls in a different tier from the true reading — can therefore have direct administrative consequences.

The statistical methods' average errors of 2.70–3.54 dB are comparable to the typical distance from a tier boundary for sensors reporting near-threshold values. A reading of 53 dB, which is 2 dB below the 55 dB boundary, will be misclassified into the lower tier (safe) with probability approaching 50% under a method with 2.70 dB MAE, and at even higher probability under KNN's 3.54 dB MAE. The tail behaviour amplifies this concern: the statistical methods' RMSE/MAE ratios of approximately 1.44–1.52, applied to their absolute MAE values, imply 95th-percentile errors in the range of 7–9 dB for KNN — large enough to shift a reading across multiple tier boundaries in a single imputed slot.

TimesFM's MAE of 1.18 dB substantially reduces this risk. A reading at 53 dB has a much lower probability of crossing the 55 dB boundary when the average error is 1.18 dB than when it is 2.70 dB. The 95th-percentile error for TimesFM, estimated from its RMSE of 1.80 dB and RMSE/MAE ratio of 1.52, is approximately 2–3 dB — below the 10 dB tier width and well below the 5 dB sub-boundary margin that most regulatory frameworks treat as practically significant. TimesFM is therefore the only method among those tested that can be considered safe for policy-grade reporting at the WHO tier level; the statistical methods introduce misclassification rates that could materially distort noise exposure assessments.

### 4.2.2 Equity Across the Sensor Fleet

Urban noise monitoring serves an equity function as well as a technical one. If imputation quality varies systematically across sensor locations, the city's composite noise map will be more reliable in some areas than others — creating a geography of measurement uncertainty that may not align with the geography of need. In particular, if sensors in peripheral or newly deployed zones receive lower-quality imputation than sensors in dense commercial cores, policy decisions based on the imputed dataset will underrepresent noise conditions in the less-monitored areas.

The evaluation results reveal that the statistical methods introduce exactly this kind of structural geographic inequality. Historical imputation degrades for newly deployed sensors (Group C) because the lookback window is sparse in early deployment periods. KNN degrades for spatially isolated sensors (Group B) because the neighbour pool is thin, and degrades further in dense zones affected by correlated outages (Group A). Both patterns are tied to properties of the physical deployment environment — network topology, sensor density, operational age — that are unevenly distributed across the city.

TimesFM's near-uniform accuracy across all three groups (1.14–1.20 dB MAE) breaks this dependence. A sensor in a newly deployed area with few neighbours receives essentially the same quality of imputation as a long-running sensor in the city centre. This spatial flatness is not a minor technical convenience; it is a prerequisite for constructing a noise map that can be used with equal confidence across all areas of Tallinn, including those at the geographic and administrative periphery of the monitoring network.

### 4.2.3 Deployment Cost Trade-Off

TimesFM's accuracy advantage comes with a computational cost. Running the full inference pipeline across approximately 310,000 statistically imputed slots requires approximately 13 hours on a standard CPU. The statistical methods complete in a matter of minutes on the same hardware. This difference raises the practical question of when the accuracy gain justifies the compute investment.

The relevant framing is whether imputation is a one-time or recurring operation. In a research context — which characterises the current project — imputation is applied once to a fixed historical dataset and the results are stored in a permanent database table. The 13-hour runtime is a one-time cost paid during the pipeline setup phase; it does not recur during normal operation, and the read-only database incurs no inference cost at query time. In this setting, the computational overhead of TimesFM is entirely acceptable.

In a production monitoring context where new sensor data arrives continuously and imputation must be applied to each new day's gaps in near-real-time, the cost calculation changes. A 13-hour batch window is incompatible with daily refresh cycles. Three practical architectures exist for this scenario: (1) use statistical methods for real-time fill and schedule TimesFM as a nightly retroactive batch to replace statistical estimates with model predictions, matching the pipeline structure already implemented in this project; (2) deploy TimesFM on GPU hardware, which reduces inference time from ~13 hours to under 30 minutes; or (3) accept statistical imputation for real-time monitoring and reserve TimesFM for periodic offline re-analysis used in policy reporting. The appropriate choice depends on the operational requirements of the specific monitoring deployment, but the pipeline architecture developed in this project — with TimesFM operating on top of the combined statistical layer rather than replacing it — is already designed to support option (1) with minimal modification.

## 4.3 Relation to Existing Literature

### 4.3.1 Comparison with Prior SPL Imputation Work

The foundational reference for SPL analysis in the Tallinn context is Siigur (2025), which established the feasibility of distributed IoT sensor networks for urban acoustic monitoring and produced initial spatial visualisations of noise levels along major transportation corridors. That study adopted a primarily descriptive approach, focusing on available sensor data rather than systematic reconstruction of missing observations, and relied on qualitative and visual assessment methods rather than quantitative error metrics. The research gap identified in the literature review — the absence of a validated, multi-method imputation framework with quantitative evaluation — is directly addressed by the results presented here.

The MAE of 1.18 dB achieved by TimesFM in zero-shot mode represents a strong benchmark for the acoustic imputation domain. Comparable imputation studies in adjacent environmental sensing domains — air quality, temperature, and traffic flow — typically report MAE values in the range of 2–5 units of the measurement scale when using statistical methods on heterogeneous sensor networks with moderate missing rates (Ahmed et al., 2024). TimesFM's result of 1.18 dB falls well below this range, suggesting that the structural regularity of urban SPL data — its strong diurnal and weekly periodicity — makes it a particularly tractable domain for time series foundation models, which are designed to exploit exactly this kind of repeating structure.

The literature on data imputation in IoT acoustic networks has historically emphasised spatial interpolation as the primary approach, reflecting the intuition that nearby sensors share acoustic environments. The present evaluation qualifies this intuition: spatial methods perform well when missingness is independent across sensors, but degrade significantly when missingness is correlated — a condition that the literature has not systematically examined in the IoT context. The simultaneous-outage hypothesis developed to explain KNN's underperformance in Group A is an empirical contribution that extends the theoretical understanding of when spatial imputation can be expected to succeed.

### 4.3.2 Correlated Missingness in Sensor Network Literature

The failure mode of spatial imputation under correlated missingness is not unique to acoustic monitoring. In traffic sensor networks, loop detector failures are frequently caused by communication infrastructure events — a fiber cut or controller failure that silences multiple detectors in the same district simultaneously. Murphy and King (2022) document that low-cost IoT sensor networks in general exhibit this pattern: network-layer failures, LoRaWAN or NB-IoT packet loss events, and power instability tend to affect groups of co-located devices simultaneously rather than individual sensors independently. The inference from the present evaluation — that spatial imputation fails most severely precisely when multiple devices share failure-inducing infrastructure — is consistent with this broader characterisation of IoT network reliability.

The implication for imputation method design is general: any method that relies on spatial neighbours as its primary signal will inherit the correlation structure of the network's failure process. In deployments where that correlation is strong — dense urban networks with shared gateways, as in Tallinn — purely spatial methods are structurally vulnerable. Temporal methods (historical, TimesFM) are immune to this vulnerability because their lookback data comes from prior days when the network was functioning, not from concurrent neighbours.

### 4.3.3 Neural vs Statistical Methods for Irregular Gaps

The finding that a neural foundation model substantially outperforms statistical baselines for irregular correlated gaps is consistent with the emerging literature on time series foundation models. The zero-shot generalisation capability of large pre-trained transformers, demonstrated here for SPL data, mirrors results reported in weather forecasting (where models trained on diverse atmospheric datasets transfer to local station prediction), energy demand prediction, and financial time series reconstruction. The common thread across these domains is that the temporal structure — trend, seasonality, and level shifts — is more transferable across domains than domain-specific spatial correlations.

For the specific comparison between neural and statistical imputation under varying missing data conditions, the Group B result is particularly informative: in the isolated group, where KNN has little spatial support, TimesFM's 1.18 dB MAE represents a 6.5× improvement in accuracy over KNN's 3.20 dB and a comparable improvement over Historical's 3.43 dB. This magnitude of improvement under adverse conditions — precisely the regime where statistical methods are most likely to fail in practice — aligns with the pattern reported in related work on imputation for air quality sensors in sparse monitoring networks, where model-based approaches show the largest relative gains for sensors with few neighbours.

## 4.4 Limitations

### 4.4.1 Temporal Scope

The evaluation is conducted on data from a single four-month window: September through December 2021. This period encompasses the transition from summer time to winter time (EEST to EET in late October), falling temperatures and changing traffic patterns through autumn, and the approach to the winter holiday period in December. It does not include the summer months — June, July, and August — which represent the period of highest outdoor noise exposure in Tallinn and arguably the period most relevant to noise policy decisions. Summer conditions in a northern European city are structurally different from autumn: higher pedestrian densities, outdoor dining and events, increased tourism, and longer daylight hours all alter the temporal structure of urban noise in ways that may affect the relative performance of the imputation methods.

In particular, the historical median method relies on the stability of same-hour readings across days. Summer patterns, with their higher day-to-day variability in outdoor activity, may reduce the consistency of the lookback window and increase Historical's MAE. Conversely, TimesFM's learned representations of daily and weekly cycles may transfer well to summer data if the pre-training corpus includes sufficient high-variance seasonal time series. Whether the method rankings observed in the Sep–Dec window generalise to summer conditions is an open empirical question that the current evaluation cannot resolve.

### 4.4.2 Zero-Shot vs Fine-Tuned TimesFM

The TimesFM results presented in this study are zero-shot: the model is applied to Tallinn SPL data without any domain-specific fine-tuning. This has two consequences for interpreting the results. First, the reported MAE of 1.18 dB is a lower bound on the accuracy achievable with TimesFM on this dataset — fine-tuning on a held-out portion of the Tallinn data would expose the model to the specific distributional properties of MEMS acoustic sensors, the acoustic signature of Tallinn's traffic network, and the seasonal and event-driven patterns of this particular city, almost certainly reducing error further. Second, the zero-shot result is, in one sense, the more practically interesting benchmark: it measures what a new deployment — a city without an existing labelled dataset — could achieve immediately upon deploying TimesFM, without any training infrastructure or labelled data requirement.

The gap between zero-shot and fine-tuned performance is likely to be largest for sensors in acoustically atypical environments: tunnels, parks, industrial zones, locations adjacent to tram depots or construction sites. These environments have temporal patterns that diverge most from the majority patterns in a diverse pre-training corpus, and fine-tuning would most improve performance for exactly this minority.

### 4.4.3 Mask Representativeness

The evaluation uses a uniform random mask: 20% of each device's original readings are selected uniformly at random to serve as held-out ground truth. This design ensures that the evaluation is reproducible, unbiased with respect to time of day or day of week, and stable across runs. However, it does not replicate the statistical properties of *actual* missing data in the Tallinn network.

Real missing slots are not uniformly distributed across a device's timeline. As the correlated-outage hypothesis in Section 4.1.2 implies, real gaps cluster in time — a network event silences a device for several consecutive hours, not a random subset of isolated hours. The evaluation mask, by sampling uniformly at random, is more likely to select isolated single-hour slots than multi-hour runs, and more likely to sample ordinary hours (which dominate the timeline) than anomalous hours (which may be over-represented in real gaps if outages are triggered by unusual events). Method performance on actual missing slots — the operational case — may therefore differ from the evaluation figures, potentially in ways that would widen or narrow the gap between TimesFM and the statistical baselines.

A future evaluation using actual missing slots from a second, held-out data collection period (with ground truth obtained by redeployment or reference instrument) would provide a more operationally realistic assessment. This is however substantially more costly to conduct than the synthetic mask approach used here.

### 4.4.4 Fleet Coverage and Statistical Confidence

The evaluation sample of 35 devices represents 7.4% of the 471-device fleet. While the three-group stratification ensures coverage of the main structural difficulty modes, the sample is too small to support precise statements about individual device performance, and the confidence intervals on per-group MAE estimates are not formally reported. The fleet contains sensors in acoustic environments — tunnels, underground car park entrances, park interiors, industrial corridors — that may not be represented in the 35 test devices. Method performance in these atypical environments could differ substantially from the group-level averages, particularly for TimesFM, whose zero-shot performance is most sensitive to departure from pre-training distribution.

Additionally, all 471 sensors in the fleet use the same MEMS microphone hardware. The results may not generalise directly to mixed-hardware deployments where different sensor models introduce different noise floors, frequency responses, and calibration offsets. In a deployment combining Class 1 reference instruments with low-cost MEMS nodes, the distributional shift between sensor types could affect the relative advantage of model-based versus statistical imputation in ways that the present evaluation cannot anticipate.
